## 스크립트

Hey, we already know there are two major types of vectors that quadrant can handle, sparse and dense. And they exist for a reason. They have different strengths. But did you know you don't need to choose just one of them? I'm Katper and I'm a senial developer advocate at Quadrant. In this video, we will discuss hybrid search that combines different approaches in a single search pipeline to boost the quality of the retrieval. But let's start with the basics. Dennis vectors are produced by neural encoders trained in a way that associates meaning with the inputs. So no matter if you ask for an elevator or a lift, your intent is to get to another floor even though the words differ. Even an escalator or just stirs should not be that far away as they serve the same purpose. Some of the models can also find what you need when you use Spanish, Polish, German or any other language as this models might be trained to support many of them simultaneously. On the other hand, sparse vectors are most suitable for cases where the exact match matters. They are quite often described as keyword-based or lexical search. Imagine you know the identifier of an item you want to find. For example, you have a particular smartphone model. So when buying some accessories, you won't want to see all the possible charges for different devices, but only those you can use. That's where lexical search would be better suited than the dense vector search. Unfortunately, reality is never that simple. Some of your users may be domain experts and use the same terminology as you do. So implementing just keyword- based search will be totally fine for their proposals. However, you don't want to ignore the part of your audience that can't express their intentions in proper terms. Some of your users may tend to produce vak natural language-like descriptions of what they want to find and in that case dense vectors will capture that way better. Imagine a legal search system. Lawyers will typically know the paragraph, section, or even a point of the legal act they want to find, while others would rather describe a very specific case they encountered. And there is much room in between. Maybe somebody knows the particular act but not the paragraph. You don't need to choose just one method or build separate pipelines to serve both types of users. Hybrid search might be the solution you are looking for. There is no single definition of hybrid search. But in general, we can call a specific pipeline hybrid when it contines at least two different search methods. So it might be dense and sparse vectors in a chain in which we prefer some candidates with dense vector search then rerank them with sparse or vice versa. So we have retrievers and reankers. The options for building a hybrid search are endless. In some cases, people create really complex multi-stage search pipelines with faster methods for the initial retrieval and slower but more precise models reanking this pre- retrieve candidates. Quadrant's universal query API allows you to build such complex pipelines in a single call. The universal query API was introduced in Quadrron 1.10. It consolidated all the other APIs in a single method and most importantly for us made it easier to build hybrid search pipelines. In this example, we prefetched 20 results with dense vector search and then sparse vectors were used to rearrange them in an order that is defined by the sparse relevancy scores. That means the sparse method was not used on all the points in the collection but just a small subset of the 20 candidates. If we swap the usage of both vectors, then we will have a different pipeline with sparse retrieval and dense reranking. Using multiple search methods in a sequence isn't always the best approach. Consider our ear examples where we either retrieve documents with dense vectors and then rerank with sparse method or retrieve with the sparse and then rerank with the dense one. These two approaches can produce completely different results. The initial retrieval method is actually more critical than the reranking step because reranking can only work with what the retriever has already selected. For instance, if there is a preferred document match that only sparse retrieval would identify, but you use dense retrieval first, you will never find that document since it won't be included in the candidates passed to the reranker. There are ways of incorporating both signals. Let's discuss fusion. Each retrieval method produces a numerical score assigned to each document it selects. These scores can't be directly compared to each other between different methods. So there is no clear way to mix candidates. For example, your dense retrieval may produce cosign similarity that never exceeds one, but BM25 scores are virtually unbounded. How would you decide whether a cosign similarity of 0.9 is better than a PM25 score of 10.7? Fusion algorithms are designed to solve that. RRF is one of the most common techniques. So let's look at it. The original scores returned by each method are primarily used to rank the documents by the relevance. So they define the order of how good each result is. Reciprocal rank fusion does not take the scores into account but just the ranking defined by them and calculates its own score based on the ranks returned by each individual method. The smaller the parameter value, the bigger the impact of the top ranked results. So let's assume you have this results coming from both methods. So, it probably captures both lexical and semantic meaning. If you would like to implement that process in quadrant, then you don't have to calculate the RRF scores on your own. It's just built in. Obviously, you can nest multiple prefetch operations and apply the fusion at any level. However, fusion is not the only way to build a hybrid search. Sometimes you have a bigger set of weak retrievers and one strong ranker which might be a different neural model such as a cross encoder that might be a more sophisticated dense retriever too which would practically be inapplicable for initial retrieval. However, there are plenty of options including multi vector representations such as colbert and maybe even some custom business rules specific to your project. Hybrid search might be really complex. Verranking is a general term for all the methods we apply to the results of some intermediate search operations and fusion is just one way of tackling it.

Today, quantum researchers around the world&nbsp; are working to make useful quantum computing&nbsp;&nbsp; a reality. The key ingredients are:&nbsp; Simulation, Improved Scale and Fidelity,&nbsp;&nbsp; Error Correction, and Hybrid Algorithms.&nbsp; Running on systems that fully integrate&nbsp;&nbsp; quantum processors with supercomputers is&nbsp; called quantum accelerated supercomputing. One promising&nbsp; approach uses individual atoms as qubits. QuEra Computing was founded about 4 years&nbsp; ago and we started out with a mission to&nbsp;&nbsp; commercialize Quantum Computing technology&nbsp; based off of neutral atom quantum bits. So,&nbsp;&nbsp; the way that we quantum compute is&nbsp; that we take individual single atoms&nbsp;&nbsp; and we cool them down to micro Kelvin&nbsp; temperatures. And then to process quantum&nbsp; information, we shine other laser beams on those&nbsp;&nbsp; atoms and we flip their electronic state to&nbsp; represent a zero or one or a zero plus one. I mean, we've known for decades that Quantum&nbsp; Computing could be a powerful thing but we also&nbsp;&nbsp; have known that decoherence and errors in qubits&nbsp; are a real-world phenomena. The main problem with&nbsp;&nbsp; quantum processors today is that they're noisy&nbsp; and there are lots of errors - you try to run&nbsp;&nbsp; a computation and you get errors. But, we have&nbsp; a way to deal with it which is called quantum&nbsp;&nbsp; error correction. For that, we need to keep&nbsp; measuring qubits in the quantum processor and&nbsp;&nbsp; then perform pretty heavy classical processing&nbsp; on the classical side. We partnered with NVIDIA&nbsp;&nbsp; about a year ago to build a new product that we&nbsp; call DGX Quantum which is a first-of-its-kind&nbsp;&nbsp; system that brings the latency between the qpu&nbsp; and the NVIDIA GPU down to the microscale and&nbsp;&nbsp; even below level. This is critical to perform&nbsp; what we call quantum error correction. This is&nbsp;&nbsp; really like the critical point for all of&nbsp; us to be able to take those noisy qubits&nbsp;&nbsp; and perfect them and create algorithms that are&nbsp; actually immune to that noise. And as we do that,&nbsp;&nbsp; there's a ripple effect up the entire stack so all&nbsp; of the ecosystem partners have to understand how&nbsp;&nbsp; this error correction works and actually design&nbsp; algorithms that take advantage of its strengths. So, hybrid algorithms are designed such that a&nbsp; quantum computer is doing something that it's&nbsp;&nbsp; good at and it's trading off with a classical&nbsp; computer that's doing something that it's&nbsp;&nbsp; good at. If you want to try and characterize a&nbsp; chemical system, understand how molecules form&nbsp;&nbsp; or behave, or how material properties emerge,&nbsp; there are a lot of hybrid algorithms that are&nbsp;&nbsp; actively being developed and deployed.

## 스크립트

Professor Dave Ancheck here. We just learned about alkanes, which are fully saturated hydrocarbons. So, now let's learn about alkenes and alkynes, which have some degree of unsaturation. The simplest alkene is ethene or ethylene, which has the formula C2H4. As you can see, there is a double bond between the carbons, which we call a degree of unsaturation. This is because it reduces the hydrogen content of the molecule because the carbons are participating in a second carbon-carbon bond, so they can only bond to two hydrogens each instead of three like with ethane. Given that this is a double bond, there is one sigma bond and one pi bond, which involve direct orbital overlap and lateral orbital overlap, respectively. The nomenclature of alkenes is essentially the same as what we learned for alkanes, but now we use the suffix -ene to indicate the presence of the double bond as we do with ethene. The other difference is that we must indicate the position of the double bond in the parent chain, as otherwise it would be ambiguous. The parent chain is numbered so as to give the double bond occurring soonest, and we specify which carbon it starts on. So, this would simply be propene as there is no other possibility for the location of the double bond since putting it in the other position would just result in the numbering starting from the other side. But with butene, we can have one butene or two butene. These are different molecules, and the number must be present in the name to indicate the position of the double bond. We should also mention the concept of cis and trans isomerism. Let's continue with two butene. We have the two carbons participating in in double bond, and then two more carbons or methyl groups. Imagine a dashed line cutting through the molecule along the axis of the double bond. If the two methyls are on the same side of this line, it's cis-2-butene. If they are on opposite sides, it's trans-2-butene. We can refer to any two substituents as being cis or trans to one another in this fashion. Moving on to alkynes, these contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond. This is ethyne, also called acetylene. Again, eth means two carbons, and yne means triple bond. Triple bonds involve one sigma bond and two pi bonds, so we end up with this propeller-looking configuration with lateral overlap of pi orbitals along two separate planes. The numbering system and nomenclature will work just like it did for alkenes, so this would be 1-hexyne. And finally, both alkenes and alkynes can be cyclic molecules, such as with cyclohexene. Here, we do not need to specify the position of the double bond because the molecule is identical no matter where it is. If substituents are present, the carbons participating in the double bond are carbons one and two in whichever order gives the substituents occurring soonest. Alkenes and alkynes participate in all kinds of chemical reactions, such as addition, hydrogenation, halogenation, and polymerization, but more details on these will have to wait for a study of organic chemistry.

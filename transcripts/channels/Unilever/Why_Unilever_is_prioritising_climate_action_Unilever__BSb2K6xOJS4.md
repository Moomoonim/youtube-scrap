## 스크립트

Why is Unilever prioritizing climate action? &gt;&gt; Scientists are warning that the world's climate has already started to reach irreversible tipping points. Impacts on people, nature, and economies are already being felt today. This poses a really significant risk to our business, our supply chains, our operations, and the people that buy our products. In recent years, we've seen commodity prices increase significantly because of drought. At the same time, severe flooding can create expensive delays to supply. To protect crop yields, we've worked with partners to increase our use of regenerative agriculture for brands such as Hellmann's and Knorr, which improve soil and water management and help safeguard farmer livelihoods against extreme weather events. We're also working hard to reduce our own greenhouse gas emissions. There's a clear business case for this. It attracts talent and partnerships. It sparks innovation, and it builds resilience to transition risks in the future, for example, carbon pricing. We focus on where we can have the biggest impact fastest, even while growing our business, whether that's in our own operations, forest risk commodities, or working with our suppliers to transition to lower carbon forms of production or raw materials. &gt;&gt; How is Unilever reducing emissions in its operations? &gt;&gt; We've set a science-based target to reduce emissions from our own operations by 100% by 2030 against a 2015 baseline. This is largely emissions from energy use across our operational sites, including factories, offices, R&amp;D labs, and data centers. As of the end of 2025, we had reduced our scope one and two greenhouse gas emissions by 77% versus our 2015 baseline. We've become more efficient in the way that we use heat and electricity across our operations. This helps lower operational cost, and also it enables a faster energy transition. One way we've achieved this is recovering waste heat and reusing that elsewhere in our processes. We've moved ahead rapidly with renewable electricity sourcing. This brings us resilience by protecting against electricity market volatility. Our main focus now is on renewable heat, but we're also looking to electrify some of our thermal processes using technology such as heat pumps and electric boilers. In addition to that, we're switching out fossil fuels for sustainably sourced biofuels. For example, in Indonesia, we're transitioning from natural gas to renewable biomethane, which is produced from waste materials from the palm oil milling process. The scale and diversity of our manufacturing network and various market complexities mean this is a considerable challenge. It's one we take very seriously. It's where we have direct impact and where we can make the fastest impact. As we make progress towards our goal, we're sharing our own learnings with our suppliers to help them do the same. &gt;&gt; How is Unilever tackling emissions from its value chain? &gt;&gt; So, Unilever has set two very ambitious science-based targets for reducing our greenhouse gas emissions across our value chain by 2030. They cover a wide range of activities outside of our own buildings. From the way we source our raw materials for our cleaning products and ingredients for our foods, right through to consumer use of our aerosols. These are not emissions that are directly emitted by us and therefore these are much more challenging to control. By the end of 2025, we have reduced our scope 3 forest, land, and agricultural emissions by 17% and our scope 3 energy and industrial emissions by 11% compared to 2021. We are absolutely determined to accelerate our progress and focus our efforts where it matters the most. For example, to reduce our emissions from forest, land, and agriculture, we have taken strides to source palm oil sustainably and we are working to maintain a deforestation-free supply chain across our major commodities. Then there is the work with R&amp;D on using low-carbon ingredients, reducing the use of plastics, as as as increasing recycled feedstocks in our products. And the third piece is how we work with our supply partners through the supply climate program and reduce their energy and industrial emissions. &gt;&gt; How is Unilever leveraging finance to scale climate solutions? &gt;&gt; Unilever's investing in climate solutions through the climate and nature fund, which is Unilever's commitment to invest 1 billion euros by 2030 in high impact climate, nature, and resource efficiency solutions. by disrupting the status quo and driving innovation. We're increasing our focus on R&amp;D, for example, in a partnership with NewFarm to develop a variety of sugarcane to extract plant-based oils for use in our cleaning ingredients. And we've invested heavily in our upstream infrastructure, bringing our palm oil refining in-house in Indonesia to help keep our supply chain deforestation-free. So, we joined forces with AXA and Tikehau to form a large regenerative agriculture private equity fund that will accelerate regenerative agriculture at scale globally. We've also partnered with HSBC to accelerate our emissions reductions and enable our supply climate program to provide lower cost sustainability-linked loans. So, it's not about waiting for the help of governments to drive this change. It's really about seeing where the gaps and the opportunities that make business sense for Unilever. &gt;&gt; How is Unilever reducing emissions from its ingredients and raw materials? &gt;&gt; To reduce emissions from chemical manufacturing, the chemicals industry must shift towards renewable energy and electrification, and we're requesting this of our suppliers. We're also looking at new ways of producing the ingredients for our products. So, in our home care business, we're looking at shifting from fossil-based ingredients to renewable and recycled ingredients to go into our products. For example, with our Sunlight Hand Dishwash, we've shifted to rhamnolipids, which is a sugar-based ingredient to deliver the surfactant that's needed to make that product work. While there are some great new innovations that we're developing with partners, the challenge is that we need to scale this at an affordable price to ensure Unilever doesn't put ourselves at competitive disadvantage and try to ensure our costs come down. In India, we're chairing a working group with many other companies and looking at material transition for the chemical industry. And through that group, developing a roadmap and some pilot projects that can show what's possible for the future. What's clear is that Unilever can't do this on its own and it can't just do it with a few suppliers. What we need is the right government policy and framework to enable the industry to transform. &gt;&gt; At Unilever, we're trying to identify and scale solutions to our decarbonization challenges as fast as we can. &gt;&gt; This will mean that the road ahead will not be a linear trajectory and there will be challenges going forward. &gt;&gt; We can do so much more together and be far more ambitious to deliver change at scale if we work with other partners. &gt;&gt; And that's why Unilever is so engaged in advocacy. &gt;&gt; Undoubtedly, climate change is a big risk to our business, but it's also a huge opportunity as well.
